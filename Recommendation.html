Recommendation

​Beginning with the teaching philosophy and methodologies of one of the most prominent instructors in the realm of nonfiction and how he shaped the curriculum of the University of Iowa, the book provides a brief yet comprehensive look at the evolution of creative nonfiction in the classroom from the 1970s onward, and how its place in academia has changed over time. While I strongly recommend Nonfiction, the Teaching of Writing, and the Influence of Richard Lloyd-Jones to English instructors and students alike, the academic value that it offers is the most beneficial to instructors of first-year writing courses. In the discourse of writing as evaluation versus writing as an opportunity to learn from taking risks, the pedagogical methods adopted by instructors adhering to the book’s suggestions cater heavily to fledgling student writers and may not be as effective when applied toward more experienced student writers. While this admittedly limits the book’s utility, it is still an invaluable resource that provides first-year writing instructors with a set of less-than traditional methods and exercises that can help brace students for the ever-changing landscape of the modern composition classroom. Additionally, the book highlights the benefits of process-centered pedagogy without completely dismissing the thought processes behind product-based pedagogy, placing context as the key for each approach.

Of course, as a creative writer, it is easy to assume that I am biased toward scholarship that serves to confirm the academic legitimacy of a topic I am passionate about. And to that end, there is no denying the work’s value as a source to instructors who have pushed for a greater emphasis on creative writing in the realm of academia, or even for instructors who recognize the contributions that Richard Lloyd-Jones has made to fuel that push. But I certainly do not intend to suggest that this book is to ignite some sort of spark to completely overhaul how first-year writing instruction is conducted. Rather, I suggest that this book be used as a supplement to traditional writing instruction. If we consider the initial discourse surrounding creative and expository writing, it is that expository writing aims to achieve a sort of practical purpose. But as the book points out, that purpose is debated – does it foster personal growth and engagement? Does it teach writing conventions valued within academia, or help students acquire rhetorical skills? (Hesse and Julier 2023). If one were to work under the assumption that expository writing aims to satisfy these concerns, then contemporary instruction that incorporates creative writing succeeds in addressing those concerns because it introduces alternative methods to developing the skills that expository writing attempts to develop. And this appears rather axiomatic – writing creatively fosters an environment of implicit learning since it evokes the same underlying cognitive processes of writing for academic contexts, with a few added affordances mentioned throughout this review. The takeaway then from this work, is that even for readers who are unfamiliar with the work of Richard Lloyd-Jones, it provides a solid overview of his instructional framework and its influence in the field of composition studies and creative writing. Additionally, it provides important context as to why creative writing has experienced a resurgence in popularity within the academy and the pedagogical strides that have been made over decades of challenging pre-established norms of composition instruction.
